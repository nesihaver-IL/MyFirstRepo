### **1. Physiological Profile & Considerations (Age 47, 70 kg)**
As a runner in the masters category (age 40+), several biological shifts occur that require specific training adjustments:
*   **Decline in VO2 Max and Speed:** VO2 max (maximum oxygen intake) typically declines by about 10% per decade, accompanied by a drop in maximum heart rate. Runners lose approximately 1% of their speed per year after age 40.
*   **The HRV Advantage:** Despite aging, well-trained masters athletes often maintain Heart Rate Variability (HRV) levels similar to inactive individuals 20 years younger. Monitoring HRV is crucial for assessing recovery and readiness for high-intensity sessions.
*   **Cardiovascular Health:** During intense effort, the heart pumps 30–35 liters of blood per minute, and systolic blood pressure can reach 200 mmHg. To manage this strain, masters runners should build a broad aerobic base in "Zone 2" to improve efficiency without overtaxing the heart.
*   **Tissue Elasticity and Muscle Mass:** Aging reduces the elasticity of tendons and collagen. Sarcopenia (muscle loss) begins to occur naturally at 3–8% per decade but can be countered with heavy resistance training.

### **2. Training Methodology & Planning**
A successful transition from 12 km to a half-marathon (21.1 km) requires a 12-to-14-week program.
*   **The 80/20 Rule:** 80% of training should be low-intensity (Zone 2, conversational pace), while only 20% should be high-intensity (intervals or tempo runs).
*   **The 10% Rule:** To prevent injury, do not increase total weekly mileage or the length of the "long run" by more than 10% per week.
*   **Key Training Sessions:**
    *   **Lactate Threshold/Tempo Runs:** These "Thinking Runner’s Workouts" are performed at a "comfortably hard" pace (approx. 83–88% of VO2 max) to teach the body to clear lactate efficiently.
    *   **The Long Run:** The most specific training for the distance. For a half-marathon, there is no need for the longest run to exceed 19 km (approx. 12 miles).
    *   **Hill Repeats:** Often described as "strength training in disguise," these strengthen legs and improve form with less impact than flat-ground sprinting.
*   **Masters-Specific Recovery:** Masters plans should utilize a **1:2 ratio**—one recovery week for every two hard training weeks—compared to the 1:3 ratio used by younger athletes.

### **3. Biomechanics & Injury Prevention**
Injury prevention is the primary goal for masters runners because recovery from tissue trauma takes longer.
*   **Optimizing Cadence:** Increasing step rate by 5–10% above the natural rate (targeting 170–185 steps per minute) reduces stride length and minimizes the impact forces on knees and hips.
*   **Avoiding Overstriding:** Runners should aim for their foot to land as close to their center of mass as possible to reduce "braking forces".
*   **Heel-Striking vs. Forefoot:** Heel-striking is not inherently "bad" unless it is associated with overstriding. Shifting to a forefoot strike can reduce knee stress but significantly increases the load on the calves and Achilles.

### **4. Strength & Conditioning (The "Must-Dos")**
Strength training is an essential requirement, not an option, for runners over 40.
*   **The Soleus (The Runner’s Muscle):** The soleus absorbs 6–8 times body weight per step. It is most active when the knee is bent. Masters runners must include **seated calf raises** (knee bent) in their routine, as standing calf raises primarily target the gastrocnemius.
*   **Glute and Core Stability:** Strengthening the glutes (via squats, lunges, and bridges) stabilizes the pelvis and prevents knee injuries like IT Band syndrome.
*   **Parameters:** Perform strength training 2–3 times per week, focusing on "heavy resistance" and progressive overload.

### **5. Nutrition & Hydration (Specific to 70 kg Body Weight)**
*   **Carbohydrate Loading:** For a 70 kg runner, the goal is to consume **8–12g of carbohydrates per kg** of body weight (560–840g daily) for 36–48 hours before a race. This maximizes glycogen stores but will lead to a 1–2 kg weight gain due to water storage.
*   **In-Race Fueling:** For efforts lasting over 60–90 minutes, consume **30–60g of carbohydrates per hour** (via gels, chews, or sports drinks).
*   **Electrolytes:** Sodium and Chloride are the primary minerals lost in sweat. Aim for **300–600 mg of sodium per hour** during long runs.
*   **Hydration Formula:** A general target is to drink half your body weight in ounces daily (approx. **1.2 liters or 40 oz for 70 kg**) as a baseline, excluding exercise-related fluids.
*   **Caffeine:** A dose of **3–6 mg/kg** (210–420 mg for 70 kg) taken 30–60 minutes before a race can significantly improve endurance performance.

### **6. Recovery & Maintenance**
*   **Sleep:** Quality sleep of **7–9 hours** is non-negotiable for repairing tissues and hormonal regulation.
*   **Stretching:** Focus on dynamic stretching before runs and static stretching (holding for 30–90 seconds) afterward to maintain joint range of motion.
*   **Pain Identification:** Distinguish between "good pain" (slight muscle soreness that fades in 24 hours) and "bad pain" (pain that keeps you awake or changes your running stride). If pain changes your stride, stop training immediately.

### **7. Tapering Strategy**
In the 2–3 weeks leading up to the race, reduce training volume by **40–60%** while maintaining race-pace intensity to ensure peak performance and "fresh legs". Strength training should be ceased roughly 10 days before the race.
